# ADR 0055: a process bounds what it takes on and how long it waits

> Status: accepted
> Date: 2026-11-16

Append-only: once merged, the body below is not rewritten. A fact later found wrong is corrected in an appended note, not edited out; a revised decision is a new ADR that supersedes this one.

## Context

Until now nothing bounded a process: a caller could send requests as fast as it liked with bodies of any size, a handler that waited on a slow dependency held its request open for ever, any number of requests ran at once, the calls to the identity provider, the decision point and the object store each carried a timeout hard-coded in their adapter or none, and a shutdown waited for every request in flight with no end. Goal #273 puts the server behind a load balancer on Kubernetes, where a pod that does not stop within its grace period (30 seconds) is killed, and where one slow dependency must not take every replica with it. PostgreSQL's pool and statement timeouts are the subject of ADR 0054 and are not repeated here.

## Decision

Each limit is a setting of the roles that serve (`api`, `runner-api`, `all`), with a default that a single process under the checks never meets.

- **Rate.** A token bucket per authenticated principal on the user API: `--rate-limit-per-second` (100) refills it and `--rate-limit-burst` (200) is its size. A request over the limit is answered 429 with `Retry-After`. Authentication comes first, so a request with no valid credential is 401 and does not spend a bucket, and one caller cannot exhaust another's. Buckets that are full again are swept, so the table holds the principals active in the last few seconds and not every principal ever seen.
- **Body size.** `--max-body-size` (1 MiB) bounds what the server reads of a request; more is answered 413. Objects do not pass through the API (ADR 0015), so this bounds JSON, not `--max-object-size`. The runner routes read up to 64 MiB, fixed, instead, because a result and an upload list every file a job produced and a refused one loses the job at its lease; at about 150 bytes a file that is hundreds of thousands of files. A runner body over that is answered 413 and the job is lost at its lease.
- **Request time.** `--request-timeout-seconds` (60) bounds a request; a request that takes longer is dropped and answered 503. Three routes are exempt from this bound alone, each for its own reason, and still count toward the concurrency cap: the runner's claim, which holds for `--runner-claim-hold` and answers when it ends; the landing of a draft; and the runner's result. What bounds a landing as a whole is the attempts after the gate, `--landing-attempts` (ADR 0036), and nothing on the wait for the gate (ADR 0039), which left the number of waiting landings unlimited; the cap on requests in flight and the caller's disconnect are all that ends a landing that waits long, and ADR 0039's sign for a landing that outwaits a proxy stands. The result is exempt because settling it may revert a landed changeset in steps (reserve the revert, land it by a compare-and-swap on `main`, record it), and a request dropped between the reservation and the record leaves the reservation and no record, so that the failing changeset is never reverted. The alternative, settling detached from the request, was not taken: the runner would be answered before the outcome is known and the error would be seen by no one. Tests pin which routes are exempt and that each is a route the router has.
- **Concurrency.** `--max-in-flight` (512) bounds the requests being served at once by an atomic counter held for the life of a request; one more is answered 503 with `Retry-After: 1`. The probes `/healthz` and `/readyz` are outside every limit and the layer does not wrap them, so a process at its cap stays ready and is not restarted for being busy.
- **Runner routes.** They are not rate limited: a runner's heartbeat and result are the protocol itself and a refused one costs the job its lease. They are bounded by the concurrency cap, the claim's hold and the heartbeat interval the server sets (ADR 0034). They have an extractor of their own, `AuthenticatedRunner`, that authenticates and does not count.
- **Timeouts of the calls a process makes.** An adapter takes its timeout from its constructor and not from a constant. `--identity-timeout-seconds` (10) covers the key set of OIDC and the endpoint of introspection, which are one provider; `--authzen-timeout-seconds` (10, at most 14) covers the decision point, garam's directory and the token client that serves them; `--s3-connect-timeout-seconds` (3) and `--s3-timeout-seconds` (10) cover the object store. The object store's timeout is a read timeout: how long to wait for a response to start and then for each next byte. It is not a total, because the whole-object `get` and `put` of landing and runs move up to `--max-object-size` (1 GiB by default) and a total of 10 seconds would cut a healthy transfer short; a transfer that stalls fails, and one that moves does not. One delivery of one entry to garam is up to four requests, and the relay claims a repository's pending entries under one lease of 60 seconds for the whole pass (ADR 0044), so the cap of 14 makes one entry's delivery (at most 56 seconds) fit in the lease, and a test of the setting's cap against the lease's constant keeps it so. It does not make a pass of several entries fit: a repository with several pending entries may outlast the lease, as it could before, and another holder may then claim them again; delivery is at least once (ADR 0044). A call that times out is an adapter's server error and is answered 5xx (ADR 0033), never a refusal of the caller.
- **Shutdown.** On SIGTERM or SIGINT the process answers `/readyz` with 503 and goes on serving for a fifth of the deadline, at most 5 seconds, so that a load balancer that probes it takes it out of rotation before it stops accepting; the 503 is a plain status with no problem body. It then stops accepting and lets the requests in flight finish, until `--shutdown-deadline-seconds` (25, below the pod's grace of 30) from the signal at the latest, when it returns and drops them. With nothing in flight it ends after the notice. A process that ends this way ends its requests without an answer, which the cap on time of a request already allows.
- **Answers.** 413, 429 and 503 are problems of the same shape as every other (ADR 0033); `Retry-After` is a header of the problem and is given where retrying helps (429, the concurrency cap) and not for a timeout, which retrying the same request does not cure. They are added to the description in `openapi.json`.

## Consequences

- The limits are per process. With `n` replicas the rate a principal may sustain is `n` times the setting, and the in-flight cap is per replica. A limit shared across replicas needs a shared store (PostgreSQL on every request, or a cache this project does not run), which #273 does not adopt; the load balancer's own limit is the place for a global one.
- A caller that stays within 100 requests a second is unaffected. A script that bursts past 200 meets 429 and is told when to retry.
- A hung dependency now ends a request in at most the request time, and the connection it held returns to the pool; before, it held both indefinitely.
- Adapter constructors take one more argument; every call site passes it, and the tests pass 10 seconds, which is what the constants were.
- `docs/guide/deployment.md` lists the new options and which roles take them; a test compares that table with each role's `--help`.
- Narrows four earlier decisions. ADR 0024's list of statuses gains 413, 429 and 503 for a timeout and for the cap. ADR 0045's "a failure reaching any other route is still 500" no longer holds: a request over the cap or past its time is 503, and a call to a dependency that times out is still 500 (ADR 0033). Its `/readyz` is also 503 for a process that is stopping, not only for a dependency that does not answer. ADR 0039's unbounded wait for the gate is unchanged but is now named as bounded by the cap only. ADR 0052's option sets gain the options above: `api`, `runner-api` and `all` take the first eight, and those and `worker` take the last two.

## Rejected

- **tower's `limit` feature** (`ConcurrencyLimit`, `RateLimit`). It pulls `tokio-util`, which the lockfile does not have, and it queues a request until a permit is free where the right answer to overload is to refuse, quickly, with a status the caller can act on.
- **504 for a request timeout.** It names a gateway, and this process is not one; 503 is already in the contract, so no status is added.
- **Rate limit by address.** Behind a load balancer every caller shares an address, and the principal is what the policy is about.
- **Rate limit runners too.** A limit on the heartbeat can make a healthy runner lose a job.
- **A timeout on every route.** The claim's hold is longer than a short timeout by design, a landing cut off half-way leaves a swap to settle (ADR 0036), and a result cut off half-way through a revert leaves the reservation without a record.
- **A total timeout on the object store.** It would cut off a healthy transfer of a large object.
- **A deadline that waits for every request.** It is the behaviour this replaces; a pod is killed at its grace period and the requests are lost without a message, where dropping them at the deadline ends them first and in order.

## Signs the decision was wrong

- Operators run more than one replica and ask for one limit across them, or a principal's legitimate burst is refused at the default.
- A request that is exempt from the timeout hangs, because its own bound does not hold.
- The 25 seconds of shutdown is not enough for the requests that finish, or the pod is killed before it ends.
